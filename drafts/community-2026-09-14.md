## 1. Reddit — r/ProductManagement

**Why this subreddit:** r/ProductManagement is where people who do prioritisation for a living
hang out, and "how many requests is enough" is a question that comes up there constantly,
usually answered with a rule of thumb someone half remembers. A founder-scale story that
actually tracked the numbers and found the rule of thumb wrong fits the sub's appetite for
concrete process detail over hot takes. Post as a text post. Keep the product mention to the
one disclosure line near the top, nothing more.

**Suggested title:**
I tracked every feature request for four months to test the "five people" rule. It nearly cost me the one that mattered.

**Body:**

I make a small iOS app called Appray, so keep that in mind reading this. Not a launch post,
just something I've been tracking since the app shipped and finally have enough data on to
write up.

Someone told me early on that the rule is five: build a feature once five people ask for it,
not before. It sounded reasonable and vaguely evidence based, so I started keeping every
request in a note to see if it held up. Four months in, seventy one messages, twenty six
distinct ideas. Only two ideas crossed five mentions.

One of them was a home screen widget. Six people asked for it, unprompted, over six weeks,
which by the rule made it an obvious yes. Except three of those six just said "a widget would
be cool," one wanted a score, one wanted a deadline reminder, and one never said what for.
Six votes turned out to be three or four different products wearing the same word.

The feature that actually mattered sat at two mentions for six weeks. Not vague ones though,
two full paragraphs describing the exact same afternoon: building one version of an answer,
wanting to try a different answer without losing the first one to compare against. Different
words, same story, nine days apart, from two people who'd clearly never spoken to each other.
I left it because two is below five and the widget looked like the bigger opportunity sitting
right above it.

Built the widget first. Three weeks of work. Usage after a month was mostly "glanced at once,
never opened again," which told me people wanted proof the score existed, not an ongoing
reason to check a widget.

While I was building it, one of the two "session" people mentioned in passing that they'd
been screenshotting their answers and manually re-typing them into a new attempt, for weeks,
because I hadn't built the thing yet. They hadn't asked again, not because they'd stopped
wanting it, but because they'd quietly built their own workaround and given up expecting me
to. That's the part a raw count can never show you: silence after a request doesn't mean the
need went away.

Shipped that one five weeks later. Four days of work. It's now one of the two or three most
used actions in the app.

What I count now: a specific story describing one real thing that happened, over three people
saying a feature sounds nice. Curious if others here have a real threshold that's worked, or
whether everyone's quietly ignoring the number the same way I ended up doing.

## 2. Quora — answering an existing question

**Question found (real, currently open on Quora):**
"How do you prioritize customers' feature requests vs. the long-term product vision?"
https://www.quora.com/How-do-you-prioritize-customers-feature-requests-vs-the-long-term-product-vision

Close enough to the blog post's actual argument (raw request counts mislead, and reading the
request matters more than tallying it) to answer directly rather than write something generic.
Check the question is still open before posting, since Quora questions occasionally get merged
or closed. Keep the product mention to one line.

**Answer draft:**

I make Appray, a decision tool that forces founders to weigh exactly this, feature requests
against everything else, for a living, so I've had to turn this from a gut feeling into
something closer to a method, mostly by getting it wrong first.

The part that changed my mind: raw request counts are a worse signal than they look. I kept a
running tally of every feature request for four months. Only two ideas out of twenty six
crossed five mentions, which is the threshold a lot of founder advice quotes as the bar for
building something. One of those two turned out to be three or four different, vaguer requests
wearing the same word (people asking for "a widget" but meaning completely different things by
it). Meanwhile a request that sat at only two mentions for six weeks, but where both people
described the exact same specific afternoon in detail, turned into one of the most used features
in the app once I finally built it.

So now I weight specificity over count. A detailed description of one real, particular moment
someone got stuck is worth more to me than five people saying a feature sounds nice in the
abstract. And I've learned to notice when a request goes quiet, because that doesn't always
mean the need disappeared. One of my two "vague count" users had quietly built their own manual
workaround and simply stopped asking, which told me the opposite of what silence usually
implies.

Long-term vision still matters and sets what you won't build regardless of how many people ask.
But within what you're willing to build, I'd trust one specific, detailed story over five vague
votes almost every time.

**Reply draft:**

I make Appray (a Build/Kill/Prove decision tool for founders), so this is close to the actual
job for me, not just a curiosity.

What's worked: I keep every request in one running note, but I stopped trusting the tally on
its own after testing it against what I actually shipped. Over four months, seventy one
messages mapped to twenty six distinct ideas, and only two crossed the "five people asked"
threshold that gets repeated as a rule of thumb. One of those two turned out to be several
different, vague requests hiding behind the same word (people asking for a "widget" but
wanting completely different things from it once I asked). A request that sat at only two
mentions, but where both people described the same specific real afternoon in real detail,
ended up being one of the most used features I've shipped.

The other thing that's mattered: a request going quiet doesn't mean the want went away.
One of the two people behind that low-count feature had quietly built their own manual
workaround (screenshotting and re-entering answers by hand) and simply stopped mentioning it,
not because they no longer needed it but because they'd given up expecting me to build it.
I'd have missed that entirely if I'd only been watching the count go up.

So: specificity over volume, and check in on requests that have gone quiet instead of reading
silence as disinterest. Raw counts are still worth noticing when they keep climbing on their
own, I just don't let them outrank a detailed story anymore.
